The Early Days: A Devastating Epidemic

In the early 1980s, the emergence of HIV/AIDS created a global health crisis. The disease was almost universally fatal, with patients typically surviving only 1-2 years after diagnosis. Stigma, fear, and misinformation surrounded the epidemic, and there were no effective treatments available.

The first antiretroviral drug, AZT (zidovudine), was approved in 1987. While it offered some hope, it had significant limitations including severe side effects and rapidly developing drug resistance. A single drug alone could not control the virus for long.

The Turning Point: HAART Era (1996)

The true breakthrough came in 1996 with the introduction of Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy (HAART) — using three or more antiretroviral drugs simultaneously. This approach, often called the "AIDS cocktail," revolutionized HIV treatment.

1987

First antiretroviral drug AZT approved by FDA

1995-1996

Protease inhibitors introduced; HAART (triple therapy) becomes standard of care

2006

First once-daily single tablet regimen approved

2012

PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis) approved for HIV prevention

2020s

Long-acting injectables (every 1-2 months) become available

The impact was dramatic. HIV viral loads became undetectable in patients, CD4 counts (immune cells) rebounded, and AIDS-related deaths plummeted. For the first time, HIV became a manageable chronic disease rather than a rapid death sentence.

Major Classes of Antiretroviral Drugs

NRTIs

Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (e.g., AZT, 3TC, Tenofovir)

NNRTIs

Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (e.g., Efavirenz, Nevirapine)

Protease Inhibitors

e.g., Ritonavir, Atazanavir, Darunavir

Integrase Inhibitors

e.g., Raltegravir, Dolutegravir, Bictegravir

Entry Inhibitors

e.g., Maraviroc, Enfuvirtide

Single Tablet Regimens

e.g., Atripla, Genvoya, Biktarvy

According to UNAIDS, AIDS-related deaths have fallen by 69% since the peak in 2004. The availability of generic antiretroviral drugs has dramatically reduced costs — from over $10,000 per patient per year in the 1990s to under $100 in many low-income countries today.

The Future: Hope for a Cure

While antiretroviral therapy is highly effective, it requires lifelong daily medication. Research continues toward:

  • Long-acting therapies — Injections every 2 months (Cabenuva)
  • HIV cure research — "Shock and kill" strategies, gene editing (CRISPR)
  • Therapeutic vaccines — Immune-based therapies to control virus without daily drugs
  • Broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) — Potential for remission without daily pills

Several cases of HIV cure have been documented through stem cell transplants (the "London Patient" and "Berlin Patient"), offering proof that a cure is possible. Research continues to translate these findings into widely applicable approaches.
